The hidden risk

The danger can follow them home.

First responders face risks most people never see. Firefighters may be exposed to smoke, soot, diesel exhaust, PFAS, benzene, formaldehyde, asbestos, contaminated gear, and other hazardous substances. Law enforcement officers and EMS professionals may face vehicle exhaust, hazardous scenes, contaminated environments, traumatic stress, infectious risks, and years of disrupted sleep.

Some risks build silently over time. A responder may feel fine, keep serving, and put everyone else first — until symptoms appear later.

Frontline Heroes Foundation exists to help move detection earlier.

The evidence

The data makes this impossible to ignore.

The strongest evidence is for firefighters — so strong that in 2022 the World Health Organization’s cancer agency (IARC) classified firefighting itself as a known human carcinogen, the same category as asbestos and tobacco smoke. The numbers show why.

The tool we fund

One scan. No radiation. A look most first responders never get.

A full-body MRI uses magnetic fields — not X-rays or radiation — to create detailed images from head to thigh in a single session, usually under an hour. It can survey many organs at once, including areas with no routine screening test today.

🧲

No radiation

Full-body MRI uses magnetic fields and radio waves — no ionizing radiation — so it can image the whole body in one sitting.

🔬

Whole-body view

A single scan can survey the brain, spine, chest, abdomen, and pelvis — including the kidneys, liver, pancreas, and lymph nodes.

⚡

Earlier signals

In studies of adults without symptoms, whole-body MRI has identified biopsy-confirmed cancers in roughly 1–2% of people, sometimes at earlier, more treatable stages.

Cancers it can help find

Guidelines already use whole-body MRI to screen or manage cancers including kidney, prostate, melanoma, multiple myeloma, and cancers in people with inherited high-risk syndromes — with growing use for ovarian cancer and lymphoma.

Why first responders

Whole-body MRI has the strongest evidence in higher-risk groups. First responders carry elevated, exposure-driven cancer risk (see the data above) — which is exactly why proactive, physician-guided imaging can matter for them.

Honest about the science: A full-body MRI is not a replacement for standard, guideline-recommended cancer screening (such as mammograms or colonoscopy), and its long-term benefit as a general screening tool is still being studied. It can also flag findings that turn out to be harmless and need follow-up. That is why every scan we fund is meant to be reviewed with a licensed physician. See our Research & Sources.
Why it matters

Earlier answers can change the path.

A full-body MRI looks for signs of cancer and other conditions before symptoms appear. For some cancers, finding disease earlier means treatment may be more effective and options may be greater.

But many first responders delay care because of cost, shift work, pride, limited access, family responsibilities, or the culture of pushing through pain.

We help remove those barriers.

The process

How the Screening Fund works

1

Donors give.

Supporters contribute to the First Responder MRI Fund.

2

3

Frontline Heroes Foundation reviews applications based on eligibility, service history, exposure risk, need, available funding, and program criteria.
