Firefighters

Firefighter cancer evidence

The evidence base is strongest for firefighters, including an IARC Group 1 classification and large federal research efforts.

IARC firefighter classification (2022)

In 2022, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC, part of the World Health Organization) classified occupational exposure as a firefighter as Group 1 — carcinogenic to humans — based on sufficient evidence in humans. Group 1 is IARC’s highest hazard classification, the same category that includes asbestos and tobacco smoke. (IARC notes this classification reflects the strength of evidence, not the size of the risk.) IARC concluded occupational exposure as a firefighter causes mesothelioma and bladder cancer, with limited evidence for cancers of the colon, prostate, and testis, melanoma of the skin, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma.

https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/iarc-monographs-evaluate-the-carcinogenicity-of-occupational-exposure-as-a-firefighter/

NIOSH/CDC firefighter cancer rates

A NIOSH/CDC study of nearly 30,000 career firefighters found a 9% increase in cancer diagnoses and a 14% increase in cancer-related deaths compared with expected U.S. population rates. The excess was driven largely by digestive, oral, respiratory, and urinary cancers.

https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2017/firefighter-cancer.html

NIOSH/CDC mesothelioma finding

In the same NIOSH/CDC research, firefighters had about twice the rate of malignant mesothelioma compared with the general U.S. population — the first U.S. study to identify an excess of this asbestos-related cancer among firefighters.

https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2017/firefighter-cancer.html

Firefighter cancer meta-analysis (bladder, prostate, testicular)

A peer-reviewed meta-analysis of cohort studies, prepared in the context of the IARC hazard evaluation, reported elevated risk among career firefighters for several cancers, including bladder (about 16% higher), prostate (about 21% higher), and testicular (about 37% higher) compared mostly with general populations.

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10300491/

IAFF occupational cancer

IAFF describes firefighter occupational cancer as the leading cause of line-of-duty death in the fire service.

https://www.iaff.org/cancer/

Firefighter Cancer Support Network

FCSN states cancer caused 66% of career firefighter line-of-duty deaths from 2002 to 2019, citing IAFF data.

https://www.firefightercancersupport.org/resources/faq

CDC/NIOSH National Firefighter Registry for Cancer

CDC/NIOSH describes the National Firefighter Registry for Cancer as the largest effort ever undertaken to understand and reduce cancer risk among U.S. firefighters.

https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/firefighters/registry/index.html

Arizona firefighter cancer presumption

Arizona Revised Statutes § 23-901.09 recognizes presumptive occupational disease coverage for firefighters and fire investigators for multiple cancers when statutory conditions are met.

https://www.azleg.gov/ars/23/00901-09.htm
Law enforcement & EMS

Law enforcement and EMS evidence

The research here is more limited, so we use careful, exposure-informed language and focus on proactive access to physician-guided screening.

Police officer cancer research

Peer-reviewed reviews say quality observational studies of cancer among police officers are sparse and limited by exposure assessment, while identifying plausible occupational risk factors.

https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/33562

University of Cincinnati law enforcement mortality study summary

A 2026 University of Cincinnati summary of a Lancet Regional Health study reported that leading causes of death among more than 15,000 U.S. law enforcement officer deaths included heart disease, cancer, suicide, and COVID-19 during pandemic years.

https://www.uc.edu/news/articles/2026/01/police-health-risks-national-study.html

CDC/NIOSH EMS worker safety

CDC/NIOSH says EMS workers face risks including infectious illnesses, hazardous chemical and body substances, and emergency transport in ground and air vehicles.

https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ems/about/index.html
Screening & full-body MRI

Early detection and the full-body MRI

National Cancer Institute — screening

NCI says cancer screening tests aim to find cancer before symptoms and when it may be easier to treat successfully, while also noting potential benefits and harms.

https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/screening-tests

American Cancer Society — screening

ACS says regular screening can often help find some cancers early before they spread and when treatment is more likely to be successful.

https://www.cancer.org/cancer/screening.html

Whole-body MRI — technique & guideline uses

Whole-body MRI is an imaging method without ionizing radiation that can cover the body with a core protocol in under 40 minutes. International guidelines recommend it for managing multiple myeloma, prostate cancer, melanoma, and individuals with certain cancer-predisposition syndromes.

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7929776/

Whole-body MRI screening in adults without symptoms

In a study of over 1,000 mostly asymptomatic adults, whole-body MRI screening detected biopsy-confirmed cancers in about 2.2% of participants — including kidney, bladder, and ovarian cancers — with many found at earlier stages.

https://radiologybusiness.com/topics/patient-care/prenuvo-reveals-early-findings-elective-whole-body-mri-clinical-trial

Whole-body MRI in higher-risk groups

In people with cancer-predisposition syndromes, whole-body MRI surveillance has detected new cancers in roughly 1 in 8 individuals (about 12%), supporting its stronger role in higher-risk populations.

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11949220/

Whole-body MRI screening — recommendations & limitations

Expert recommendations note whole-body MRI screening is best targeted to higher-risk individuals. In people without symptoms it commonly finds incidental abnormalities that are ultimately benign but require follow-up, and its long-term screening benefit and cost-effectiveness are still being evaluated.

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8558201/
Accountability

Nonprofit governance & privacy standards we follow

IRS tax-exempt organization search

IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search lets users check an organization’s eligibility to receive tax-deductible charitable contributions.

https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/search-for-tax-exempt-organizations

IRS 501(c)(3) requirements

Organizations described in section 501(c)(3) are commonly called charitable organizations and are generally eligible to receive tax-deductible contributions under Code section 170.

https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations/exemption-requirements-501c3-organizations

IRS written acknowledgment

IRS written acknowledgments for charitable contributions of $250 or more must include the organization name, contribution amount or non-cash description, and whether goods or services were provided.

https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations/charitable-contributions-written-acknowledgments

HHS HIPAA covered entities

HHS explains privacy and security obligations for covered entities and business associates.

https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/covered-entities/index.html

FTC consumer health information

FTC guidance says consumer health information can include more than diagnoses and treatments, including information that enables health inferences.

https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach
